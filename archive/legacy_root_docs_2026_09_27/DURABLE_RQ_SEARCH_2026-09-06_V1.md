# 1. Search doctrine after reading Slack #r_hamdi

The strongest lesson from #r_hamdi is not merely "use simple synthetic data."

It is:

> **Choose a scientific object before choosing an effect.**

The real/fictional project asks whether the model's representation of an entity's
ontological status is distinct from:
- whether the entity is familiar;
- whether the model knows facts about it;
- whether a proposition about it is true;
- whether a prompt asks the model to play along.

The random-choice project asks whether the model has an internal state corresponding
to arbitrary-choice mode and how that state is read, written, and causally used.

Neither research question requires a particular anomaly to exist.

The later findings:
- real/fictional linear geometry;
- contextual rigidity;
- behavior/representation dissociation;
- random-choice reader/writer decomposition;
- switch/dial behavior;
- low-rank causal edit;

are **answers generated by the object**, not the definition of the object.

This is the model for the present search.

# 2. What we are looking for

Prefer questions of the form:

> **There is a basic state/computation that a language model must plausibly maintain
> across many tasks, but current work has not established how it is represented,
> distinguished from neighboring states, causally used, or changed by post-training.**

A good object should support several independent research axes:

1. behavior;
2. representation;
3. cross-domain generalization;
4. dissociation from nearby concepts;
5. causal use;
6. training-stage differences;
7. intervention;
8. downstream agent consequence.

The data should still be Figure-1 simple.

---

## 3.1 Research Question

> **Do language models represent socially conferred institutional status as a state
> distinct from the underlying physical facts and from the rules/norms that give that
> status its consequences?**

Shorter:

> **How do language models represent social reality that is true by institution rather
> than by physical state?**

---

## 3.2 Why this matters

A large part of the world described and acted on through language is neither:

1. a brute physical fact; nor
2. a normative statement about what someone ought to do.

It is an **institutional fact**.

Examples:
- who owns an object;
- who is a member of a group;
- who holds an office;
- whether a token counts as valid money/ticket/credential;
- who has an institutional status that carries rights or powers.

Such facts can remain true while the physical substrate changes, and the same physical
configuration can have different social status under different institutions.

For an agent, confusing physical state with institutional state can change:
- authorization;
- transfer rights;
- obligations;
- coordination;
- tool actions;
- interpretation of declarations;
- long-horizon state tracking.

This is a persistent computational object, not a benchmark trick.

---

## 3.3 One-example test

> Lena is holding Omar's bicycle because Omar lent it to her.

Lena currently **possesses** the bicycle.  
Omar still **owns** it.

A reader immediately understands that physical control and institutional ownership are
different states.

A second example:

> Nora was elected chair of the committee, but Eli is sitting in the chair's seat.

The physical location does not determine who holds the office.

---

## 3.4 Scientific object

The object is not "ownership accuracy."

It is the model's representation of **institutional status**: a socially constituted
state that is supported by social rules/practices and can carry downstream deontic
powers.

Classical social ontology describes a structure such as:

brute / substrate fact  
→ constitutive condition or social recognition  
→ institutional status  
→ rights / obligations / permissions / authority

The empirical question is whether language models compute anything structurally like
this, or whether they instead reduce each domain to local lexical/world-knowledge
associations.

---

## 3.5 Nearby distinctions that must not be confused

### Physical possession vs ownership
Holding/using an object is not the same as owning it.

### Role label vs institutional status
A prompted persona such as "act as a manager" is not the same scientific object as
someone becoming the manager under a social/institutional process.

### Constitutive rule vs regulative rule
A constitutive rule determines what **counts as** a status.

A regulative/deontic rule determines what is:
- obligatory;
- permitted;
- prohibited.

The candidate is about the state created/recognized by the former and its relation to
the latter.

### Authority cue vs status
A model treating "doctor" as high-authority is not the same as representing that a
specific person currently holds an office/power.

### Social norm vs institutional fact
"People should queue" is normative.

"Maya is the registered member" is an institutional fact.

### Social commonsense vs status representation
Behavioral knowledge that people borrow things is not enough. The question concerns
the internal factorization and causal use of the resulting social state.

## 3.6A Decodability is explicitly NOT a discovery criterion

A direct lesson from advisor feedback in Slack `#r_hamdi` is now a hard constraint on
Candidate IF.

Sasano's comparison was roughly:

- a shared true/false direction across very different domains and statement types can
  be scientifically interesting because those examples have substantially different
  surface distributions;
- by contrast, semantically different frames clustering in representation space is
  much less surprising, because the manipulation itself changes meaning and prior work
  already suggests such clustering.

Applied here:

> **A probe that separates owner/non-owner, member/non-member, chair/non-chair, or
> authorized/unauthorized states is not by itself evidence for a new scientific
> object.**

Those labels differ semantically and may be trivially decodable from lexical or local
contextual cues.

For institutional status to become interesting, at least one stronger structural
property must appear:

1. **Factorization:** status remains decodable when physical substrate is crossed
   independently with status, rather than when status words differ.
2. **Cross-domain structure:** a representation learned from one or more status domains
   predicts structurally analogous states in held-out domains beyond lexical semantics.
3. **Dynamic persistence:** the status state changes at constituting/revoking events and
   remains stable across irrelevant physical-state changes.
4. **Causal mediation:** intervening on the status state changes status-derived
   consequences while preserving matched physical facts.
5. **Training dissociation:** pretraining/post-training changes whether the state is
   used, not merely whether role/status vocabulary is recognized.

If the best result is "status labels are linearly separable," **KILL**.

This criterion is deliberately stronger than ordinary mechanistic-probing work and is
required to avoid reproducing the exact research mistake the advisor warned about.


# 4. Outcome-Robustness Test

The question is intentionally not defined by one desired anomaly.

| plausible outcome | still useful? | what would be learned? |
|---|---|---|
| physical substrate and institutional status are cleanly separable internally | **yes** | LMs maintain a factorized social-world state rather than only surface associations |
| status is represented, but only domain-specifically | **yes** | social ontology may be a collection of learned relational schemas, not one general status axis |
| behavior is correct but status is not represented independently | **yes** | models may recompute social conclusions from local text/rules rather than maintain a stable institutional state |
| status is internally present but downstream rights/permissions do not follow reliably | **yes** | representation and deontic use dissociate |
| base and instruction-tuned models represent status similarly but use it differently | **yes** | post-training may change the policy/readout rather than social-state representation |
| novel/nonce institutions fail while familiar ones work | **yes** | apparent social-state competence may be memorized convention rather than general constitutive reasoning |
| no stable distinction appears even within familiar domains | **yes, but probably a negative paper lead only if very systematic** | LM social-state representations may be deeply entangled with physical/lexical features |
| one tiny behavioral bias appears in one domain | **no** | kill; this would not justify the broad object |

This is materially more outcome-robust than zero/nonzero or a narrow ordering effect.

# 5. Research-Space Map

If the object survives, the research program can grow along several independent axes
without changing the question.

## Axis A — Behavioral factorization

Can the model keep apart:
- who physically has X;
- who owns X;
- who is physically present;
- who holds a role;
- who holds a credential;
- who has the status the credential signifies?

The goal is not leaderboard accuracy. It is to establish whether the two state
dimensions can be varied independently.

## Axis B — Representation

Ask whether the model encodes:
- physical substrate;
- institutional status;

as separable components.

Possible tools:
- linear probes;
- low-rank subspaces;
- representational similarity;
- cross-condition decoding;
- cross-domain alignment.

Do not assume a single universal "institutional vector."

A negative result about one global direction does not kill the object.

## Axis C — Cross-domain generalization

Potential shallow domains:
- ownership;
- membership;
- office/role holding;
- authorization/credential status;
- valid ticket/token;
- institutional relationship.

The scientific question is whether similar structural computations recur across
different social statuses.

## Axis D — Familiar vs newly constituted institutions

After familiar social domains are understood, introduce a **minimal nonce institution**
with an explicit constitutive rule.

This is analogous to Hamdi's nonce entities:
it tests whether the mechanism constructs a new status online rather than only recalling
familiar social facts.

This stage is not allowed to become a complicated world ontology.

## Axis E — Dynamic status update and persistence

Institutional states can change through:
- transfer;
- appointment;
- enrollment;
- revocation;
- validation;
- resignation.

Ask whether the internal status updates when the constituting event occurs and persists
through irrelevant changes in physical state.

## Axis F — Deontic consequences

Many institutional statuses carry:
- rights;
- permissions;
- obligations;
- powers.

Ask whether the status representation mediates these consequences, instead of the model
matching a local rule independently on every query.

## Axis G — Causal mechanism

If a status representation is identified:
- interchange it;
- patch it;
- ablate it;
- steer it.

A strong intervention would change status-derived judgments while preserving matched
physical facts.

This would be much stronger than merely decoding "owner" or "member."

## Axis H — Training

Compare:
- base model;
- instruction-tuned model;
- optionally reasoning/post-trained variants.

Question:
does post-training create the social-status representation, or mainly change whether it
is used in behavior?

# 6. Minimal Discovery Substrate — shape only, NOT an authorized experiment

The preferred starting shape is a simple factorial within familiar domains.

Example ownership quartet:

1. physically possesses + institutionally owns;
2. physically possesses + does not own (borrowed);
3. does not physically possess + owns (lent out);
4. neither possesses nor owns.

The corresponding story can fit in one or two sentences.

Analogous independent factors can be constructed for office, membership, and
credential/token domains.

The purpose of this 2×2 is not to discover an A/B accuracy gap.

It is to create conditions in which **physical state and social status are identifiable
independently** for behavioral and representational analysis.

No target-model pilot is authorized until the exact substrate passes a separate
naturalness/gold audit.

---

# 7. Gold

Gold should come from explicit ordinary events, not external databases or LLM judges.

Example:

> Omar bought the bicycle and has not sold or given it away. He lent it to Lena for the
> afternoon. Lena is riding it now.

Physical possession: Lena.  
Ownership: Omar.

The scenario itself determines the gold.

Avoid:
- disputed legal edge cases;
- jurisdiction-specific law;
- inheritance;
- contested contracts;
- complex corporate governance;
- intellectual property.

The project should study the basic computational distinction, not legal doctrine.

# 8. Novelty Threat Map

The current search has **not** found a modern LLM paper whose central object is:

> a cross-domain internal representation of institutional status, factorized from
> physical substrate and causally linked to status-derived consequences.

That statement is a search result, not proof of novelty.

Strong neighbors:

## Threat 1 — Social-role representation

**The Granularity Axis: A Micro-to-Macro Latent Direction for Social Roles in Language
Models** (2026) studies the latent geometry of prompted social roles.

Kills:
> "LLMs internally represent social roles."

Does not currently appear to study:
> status conferred by constitutive social events/rules vs physical substrate.

## Threat 2 — Role-conditioned access control

**Role-Conditioned Refusals** (Findings of EACL 2026) evaluates whether different
database roles are correctly permitted/refused.

Kills:
> "LLMs struggle with role permissions."

It is an explicit RBAC-policy benchmark, not a study of institutional-status
representation across social ontology.

## Threat 3 — Authority / rule-compliance mechanisms

A 2026 mechanistic study/blog, **Reading and Writing Rule-Compliance Representations in
LLMs**, begins from authority but finds a more general rule-action applicability signal.

Kills:
> "we discovered an authority vector."

This is a major threat to any permission/action-applicability claim.

A surviving institutional-status paper must show that the state exists **before and
apart from** local action-policy matching.

## Threat 4 — Legal constitutive-rule classification

Liga & Robaldo (2023), **Fine-tuning GPT-3 for legal rule classification**, classifies
obligation, permission and constitutive rule texts.

Kills:
> "LLMs can distinguish constitutive from deontic rules."

But it studies rule-text classification, not the internal world state resulting from a
constitutive rule.

## Threat 5 — General social knowledge

SocKET (EMNLP 2023) and later social-reasoning benchmarks cover broad social language
and commonsense.

Kills:
> "LLMs have not been evaluated on social knowledge."

The candidate must be about a specific state/computation.

## Threat 6 — Persona/social-agent architectures

2026 work on LLM-enabled social agents argues that roles, norms, commitments and
institutional structure should be represented in agent architectures.

This strengthens the practical importance of the object but raises the novelty bar:
the paper cannot merely propose "agents should track roles."

It must empirically characterize how the base model represents socially constituted
state.

## Threat 7 — Speech-act felicity

Speech-act/declaration work and FelicityBench-like efforts can overlap with status
creation through authorized declarations.

Therefore:
> "authorized utterances can change status"

is not sufficient novelty.

The institutional-status object must generalize beyond speech acts.

## Threat 8 — Social World Models (2025–2026)

Recent work already claims the phrase **social world model**.

- *Social World Models* (2025/2026) introduces S3AP, an explicit representation of
  agent state, observations, actions and mental states, then uses a model to predict
  how social interaction evolves.
- *Building Social World Models with Large Language Models* (ICML 2026) models how
  aggregate social beliefs change after real-world events.
- 2026 mechanistic work asks whether Theory of Mind and pragmatic reasoning recruit a
  shared social-world-model computation.

This kills any broad framing such as:

> "Do LLMs have a social world state/model?"

A direct inspection of the S3AP paper shows that its load-bearing represented states
are primarily agents' beliefs/intentions, observations, actions and interaction
dynamics. It does not center institutional statuses such as ownership, office,
membership, currency/token validity, or constitutive status transitions.

Therefore IF can survive only as a **specific missing component of social world
modeling**:

> socially constituted state that exists because an entity/person counts as holding a
> status in a social/institutional context, not merely because of its physical
> properties or an agent's private mental state.

Do not use "social world model" itself as novelty language.

## Threat 8 — Generic relational-concept representations

NAACL 2024 **Identifying Linear Relational Concepts in Large Language Models** and
Findings ACL 2024 **Locating and Extracting Relational Concepts in Large Language
Models** already show that relation-level hidden representations can be identified,
causally mediated, transplanted, and rewritten.

This kills a weak mechanistic story such as:

> "ownership / membership / office relations are linearly decodable or causally
> editable."

That would be a domain instantiation of existing relational-representation work.

Candidate IF survives only if the contribution is specifically social-ontological:
- status is factorized from the physical/substrate state;
- creation/revocation events update status while irrelevant physical features stay
  fixed;
- analogous structure transfers across multiple institutional domains or novel
  institutions;
- status causally mediates downstream rights/powers rather than merely recalling a
  subject–relation–object fact.

Generic relation vectors are a **methodological baseline/threat**, not novelty.

## Threat 9 — Social ontology philosophy

Searle, Hindriks, Marmor and others have studied institutional facts/status functions
for decades.

The **human/philosophical distinction is not new**.

The novelty, if any, must be a new empirical law about language-model representations
and computation.

---

# 9. Reviewer kill sentence

The candidate must defeat:

> **"This is just social commonsense / role reasoning / policy compliance with
> mechanistic probes."**

A paper can defeat that only if it shows a structural result such as:

- physical and institutional state are independently represented;
- the status component generalizes across social domains or newly introduced
  institutions;
- the status state causally mediates downstream consequences while physical-state
  knowledge is held fixed;
- training changes the readout/use of this status rather than merely memorizing role
  labels.

If we cannot get beyond per-domain classification, kill.

---

# 10. Non-triviality Test

These findings are **not** sufficient:

> owners have more permissions than borrowers;

> a manager can approve things;

> wearing a badge suggests membership;

> explicitly stating a role improves role reasoning.

Those are obvious.

The scientific payoff must concern:
- factorization;
- generality;
- dynamics;
- causal use;
- training;
- dissociation.

# 11. Candidate Growth Path

A healthy paper could look like:

### C1 — Social-state representation

Language models separate socially conferred status from matched physical substrate
across several ordinary domains.

OR another comparably strong observed structural law.

↓

### C2 — Generality / dissociation

Characterize whether:
- the state transfers across domains;
- it can be constructed for novel institutions;
- it persists across irrelevant physical changes;
- it dissociates from the rule text, role label, and downstream norm.

↓

### C3 — Causal role

Intervening on the status state selectively changes status-derived conclusions/actions
without changing the underlying physical facts.

Potential training comparison:
base vs instruction tuning may change use rather than representation.

This is a genuine behavior → structure → causal-use descent.

# 12. Pilot Kill Criteria for IF

Do not authorize even a small pilot if continued literature search finds:

1. a modern LLM study already factorizing institutional/social status from physical
   substrate across domains;
2. a mechanistic social-role paper whose core claim is already a domain-general
   status-function representation;
3. a constitutive-reasoning benchmark that directly studies status creation, persistence,
   and status-derived deontic consequences as one object.

After a future pilot, kill as mainline if:

4. the task reduces to lexical role labels;
5. performance is just social-commonsense QA;
6. representation is no more informative than the explicit rule sentence;
7. only ownership works and other status domains do not;
8. the only interesting result is one small bias/error;
9. a complex artificial legal/institutional ontology is required;
10. the result can be completely restated as rule-action applicability.

# 14. Other current durable-search lanes

## 14.1 Epistemic vs world-changing action mode

### Verdict: HIGH-RISK / DOWNGRADED

Initial RQ:
> Do LLM agents represent whether an action changes only their information state or
> changes the external world?

Why it looked strong:
- persistent across tools and environments;
- directly relevant to autonomy/safety;
- representation → planning → intervention path.

Why it was downgraded:
- agent benchmarks already distinguish read-only/write/irreversible actions;
- 2026 work explicitly studies reversibility classes;
- mechanistic work now decodes pre-action tool necessity and action consequentiality;
- tool-call propensity itself is steerable by a linear direction.

Reviewer compression:
> another mechanistic tool-risk/side-effect representation.

**Do not promote.**

---

## 14.2 Category membership vs typicality

### Verdict: HIGH-RISK SEARCH LEAD

RQ shape:
> Does the model distinguish "is a member of category C" from "is a typical example of
> C"?

Why interesting:
- membership and prototype similarity need not coincide;
- a model trained on distributional statistics could entangle them;
- representation and causal-use questions are broad.

Why not strong enough:
- prototype/typicality representation is already an active LLM research topic;
- cognitive science itself debates graded category membership for natural categories;
- natural gold can be ambiguous;
- artificial categories risk becoming generic rule induction.

**No pilot.**
