## Why this pilot matters

G29B found that a semantically relevant but never-admissible contradictory sentence changes later 0–9 truth ratings relative to a similarly excluded irrelevant sentence. That result could depend on natural Wikipedia revision semantics or on the rating task. G30 asks the same admissibility question in a different inferential environment: a prior over Blue/Yellow, a diagnostic report with stated reliability, a single final admissible report E2, and an exact Bayesian posterior. The final response is an explicit probability forecast and a Blue/Yellow decision. The 40 cells are the complete factorial of five priors, four reliabilities, and two E2 directions. This is a controlled anchor, not a natural dataset or a new benchmark.

## Main observations

| Contrast, pooled across three models | Result | Reading |
|---|---:|---|
| Never-admissible relevant E1 **N** minus never-admissible irrelevant E1 **I**, signed toward E1 | +0.16 pp [−1.18,+1.57] | No common directional content effect in probability forecasts |
| Absolute N−I probability difference | 3.91 pp [2.44,5.69] | Some case-specific sensitivity remains |
| N/I direct-choice disagreement | 4% [1%,7%] | Decision changes are uncommon here |
| Initially valid then revoked **R** minus N, signed toward E1 | +6.38 pp [4.67,8.05] | A pooled statistic dominated by a Gemma-specific prior-copying mode; not a common graded retraction effect |
| Bayesian absolute error, D / I / N / R | 7.49 / 8.69 / 10.05 / 14.20 pp | R has the largest error; paired R−N = +4.15 pp [2.45,5.82] |

The pooled N−I near-zero is genuinely heterogeneous: Qwen −2.55 pp, Gemma +4.13 pp, and Mistral −1.10 pp. It **does not reproduce G29B's common negative N−I direction**. Even the formal task is not perfectly content-invariant item by item, but this is far below G29B's 14% N/I direct-choice disagreement, and the current direct choice effect has no shared polarity.

R−N varies sharply: Qwen −0.71 pp [−5.31,+2.65], Gemma +15.86 pp [12.74,18.99], Mistral +3.98 pp [2.52,5.53]. A post hoc read of the full factorial shows that **Gemma's R output equals the prior in 32/40 cells and equals either the prior or its complement in 40/40**. For every prior × E2 combination, its R forecast is exactly constant across reliabilities 65%, 75%, 85%, and 95%; the final valid E2 separation is 18.00 pp at **each** reliability. In N, Gemma's E2 separation rises from 43.33 to 58.40 pp across those reliabilities. Qwen and Mistral show nonconstant R forecasts and different R−N patterns. Thus the pooled +6.38 pp is substantially a **model-specific prompt-processing or prior-copying mode**, not good evidence for a shared, graded retraction bias or direct reuse of E1. Qwen has one large outlying R/N pair (N=100, R=25 for a 5% Bayesian posterior); it remains in every estimate.

## What changed in our explanation

The results resist a single directional rule. Natural same-claim evidence in G29 showed R≈N, while N differed from I on graded ratings, with stronger uptake of contradictory E2 in N. Here, N≈I on the signed aggregate, while the apparent R−N effect largely reflects Gemma's discrete response mode. Thus neither “revocation status is always irrelevant” nor “excluded contradictory content always causes contrast enhancement” survives transfer unchanged. G30's three models appear to have different failure modes; pooling them obscures this structure.

A stronger question is whether selective evidence control can **suppress disallowed X, restore the appropriate judgment, and preserve normal use of still-valid E2**. These are separate measurements, detailed in the [working framework](../discovery/final_admissible_set_sufficiency_v1.md). ConfB directly establishes strong polarity suppression alongside poor same-item restoration. G29B and G30 offer exploratory evidence that valid-E2 response profiles can change under excluded histories. G30's most dramatic change belongs to Gemma's prior-copying regime and cannot support a shared preservation law. The varying signs across experiments rule out one fixed residual direction as a unifying explanation.

The formal pilot has a clear limitation: its “Turn 1 / Turn 2” histories are **rendered in one user message**, rather than executed as a multi-turn chat. R is also longer than N because the random revocation is spelled out. The R−N contrast therefore cannot yet be attributed uniquely to a revocation event rather than the processing cost or framing of the longer history. The 40 cells are a designed grid, not independent natural examples. Verbalized probability remains an elicited number, albeit one with a normative answer and ordinary forecasting semantics. Mistral's existing tokenizer emits a known regex warning; this run used the same checkpoint/environment family as G29 and retained all its rows.

## Research decision

Keep the confirmed ConfB suppression–restoration gap as the empirical center. Treat G29B's “never-admissible content modulates later evidence use” as a natural-evidence candidate with model heterogeneity. G30 does **not** confirm that direction; its strongest R pattern is a Gemma-specific prior-copying failure. Preservation of valid-evidence sensitivity is the thinnest part of the three-part framework. Do not elevate G30's pooled R−N as a shared retraction effect.

The most valuable next experiment is a **single natural item family measuring suppression, restoration, and preservation together**: matched X polarity, clean and excluded histories, and crossed valid-E2 levels. That can test whether apparent success at suppression coexists with collateral damage to E2 use on the same item/model. Another D/N/I/R domain alone would not answer that question. A small multi-turn variant of G30 is useful only if we want to study Gemma's prompt-processing mode; it is not required to establish the ConfB core.

Relevant prior: [Gonçalves et al.](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article/93/1/476/8159675) already use a never-observed comparator and examine later updating in humans; [Kim et al., NAACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.531/) already study LLM confidence under evidence informativeness, reliability, and irrelevance; [Tsujimura et al., ACL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1860/) already evaluate belief consistency during context progression; [Kumaran et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-026-01217-9) already show LLM overweighting of opposing advice; [Jang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18505) and [Unlearning as Distribution Restoration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19442) already distinguish suppression from a stronger restoration/deletion criterion in other settings. Our proposed contribution must stay specific to explicit in-context evidence eligibility, apparent suppression success, paired same-item restoration, and use of later valid evidence.
