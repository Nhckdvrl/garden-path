\title{Language Models Keep Abandoned Analyses:\\ Residue and Adaptation after Garden-Path Sentences}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{abstract}
Reading \ex{While the man hunted the deer ran into the woods} requires giving up the analysis in which \ex{the deer} is the object of \ex{hunted}.
People who have just read such a sentence still produce more transitive structures, so the abandoned analysis primes later language.
We ask whether language models keep using an analysis they have abandoned, and what in the model carries it.
In 24 open models from 124M to 70B parameters, a garden-path sentence leaves two traces in the context.
A residue of the abandoned analysis raises later object attachment, by about three quarters of the effect of a sentence in which the noun phrase really is an object, and it appears in sampled text, on held-out and human-normed items and for a second type of garden path.
An adaptation toward the resolved structure scales with the sentence's disambiguation error and acts mainly on comprehension-like measures.
Activation patching separates the two.
Later text reads the residue from the role information in the noun phrase's states, which were computed under the object analysis before the disambiguation, and replacing these states removes almost all of the residue in the 32B and 70B models.
The adaptation is read from the states that processed the disambiguation.
Overridden statements show the same persistence (a corrected value keeps pulling later predictions in 8 of 9 models), and in most models the pull is carried by the positions after the statement.
Finally, the human paradigm transfers only with controls for punctuation, because the models also write garden-path sentences themselves, which reverses the paradigm's raw statistic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Garden-path sentences make a reader build an analysis and then give it up.
In \ex{While the man hunted the deer ran into the woods}, the noun phrase \ex{the deer} is first attached as the object of \ex{hunted}, and the verb \ex{ran} reveals that it is the subject of the main clause \citep{frazier1982}.
In language models (LMs), which have become a standard test bed for such sentences, surprisal at the disambiguating word predicts that garden-path effects occur but underestimates their size \citep{futrell2019,vanschijndel2021,arehalli2022,huang2024}.
Asked \ex{Did the man hunt the deer?}, they often answer yes, like human readers who keep part of the initial interpretation \citep{christianson2001,li2024incremental,amouyal2025}.
These studies examine the garden-path sentence itself, either the difficulty of its disambiguation or the accuracy of its final interpretation.

The abandoned analysis can also shape what comes after the sentence.
\citet{vangompel2006} had participants read a garden-path sentence and then complete a fragment such as \ex{While the nurse washed}.
Completions were more often transitive after the garden path than after a version disambiguated by a comma (33.6\% against 29.1\% in their first experiment), so the object analysis that had been ruled out still primed later production.
For LMs this question has a specific architectural form.
A causal transformer computes the representation of each token when the token is read, and later tokens retrieve these representations through attention.
When \ex{ran} arrives, the states of \ex{the deer} have already been computed under the object analysis, and they stay in the context unchanged.
The states computed after the disambiguation, in contrast, can encode the corrected analysis.
Whether later text is shaped by the abandoned analysis therefore depends on which of these states it reads, and this cannot be decided from the architecture alone.

Studies of structural priming in LMs have so far not been able to address this question.
LMs reuse the structure of a preceding sentence \citep{sinclair2022,prasad2019,michaelov2023,pucci2026}, and they do so more after less expected primes, an inverse-frequency effect that \citet{zhou2025icl} take as evidence that in-context learning is error-driven \citep[see also][]{jumelet2024}.
In human research the same effect separates two accounts of priming.
Residual activation holds that a processed structure stays active and is reused because it is active \citep{pickering1998}.
Error-driven learning holds that a prime adjusts expectations in proportion to its prediction error \citep{chang2006,jaeger2013}.
With unambiguous primes, both accounts predict priming of the prime's structure, and only the scaling with prediction error tells them apart.

Garden-path primes add a second contrast.
For the abandoned object analysis, residual activation predicts reuse that grows with how strongly the analysis was built.
Error-driven learning predicts the opposite tendency, because the error occurs when the object analysis fails, and learning from it should favour the resolved structure in which \ex{the deer} starts a new clause.
Human data show only the net result.
LMs let us measure the effect of a garden-path prime on later text, relate it to the strength of the abandoned analysis and to the disambiguation error, and locate in the context what carries each part.

We pair garden-path primes from five published item sets with target fragments and measure how a prime changes a production-like quantity, the preference for attaching the target's noun phrase as an object, and a comprehension-like quantity, the surprisal of the disambiguating word in a garden-path target.
Across 24 models we compare primes matched for punctuation and verb, fix the predictions of each confirmatory experiment before collecting its data, and use activation patching to locate the effects inside the prime.

A garden-path prime raises later object attachment by about three quarters of the effect of a prime whose noun phrase really is an object, and most of this residue requires a noun phrase that could have been an object.
The same prime produces an adaptation toward the resolved structure that grows with its disambiguation error and acts mainly on the comprehension-like measure, and patching shows that later text reads the two from different positions of the prime.
To see how far the persistence extends, we also test statements that a later sentence corrects.
The overridden value keeps influencing later predictions, but through a different route than the garden path.

Our contributions are as follows.
(1) We show that LMs carry an abandoned syntactic analysis into later production, across 24 models, three item sets and two types of garden path, with controls that rule out punctuation, verb bias and local string overlap as its source.
(2) We show that the same prime also produces an adaptation toward the resolved analysis, that the two effects scale with different properties of the prime (the strength of the abandoned analysis and the disambiguation error), and that their balance differs between production-like and comprehension-like measures.
(3) We show by activation patching, probing and a role-direction intervention that later text reads the abandoned analysis from the role information in noun-phrase states computed before the disambiguation, even though the states after it encode the reanalysis.
(4) We show that overridden statements also persist, in most models through later positions, and that the human paradigm's statistic reverses in LMs unless punctuation is controlled.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Garden paths in language models.}
Garden-path sentences were among the first constructions used to ask whether neural LMs track syntactic state \citep{futrell2019}.
Surprisal from such models predicts that the effects exist but underestimates their size and misorders constructions \citep{vanschijndel2021,arehalli2022,huang2024}.
Recent work relates this gap to the number of parses a model keeps alive \citep{timkey2026} or closes it with suitably trained models \citep{yoshida2026}.
Comprehension studies ask whether the final interpretation is correct, and find that LMs often accept the initial misreading, with plausibility and verb type modulating this as in humans \citep{li2024incremental,amouyal2025}, and difficulty with such structures persists across model scale \citep{amouyal2026}.
\citet{hanna2025} found features for both readings of a garden-path prefix in small models and showed that the circuit that answers comprehension questions hardly reuses them.
None of this work asks what the abandoned analysis does to later text.

\paragraph{Structural priming and adaptation.}
Speakers reuse structures they have recently processed \citep{bock1986,pickering1998}.
Residual activation and error-driven learning \citep{chang2006} are the two main accounts, and computational models combine short-lived activation with longer-lived learning \citep{reitter2011}, in line with a lexical boost that decays while abstract priming persists \citep{hartsuiker2008}.
Less expected primes prime more \citep{jaeger2013}, and readers adapt to the main-verb/reduced-relative garden path with exposure \citep{fine2013}, although such rapid adaptation is small and hard to replicate \citep{harringtonstack2018,prasad2021}.
\citet{vangompel2006} found priming of the abandoned analysis of NP/Z garden paths, and \citet{fujita2020} found that unambiguous primes ease the reading of later garden paths without removing lingering misinterpretation.
In LMs, conditioning on a prime raises the probability of its structure in a target \citep{prasad2019,sinclair2022}, across languages \citep{michaelov2023} and in generation \citep{pucci2026}.
The effect grows for less expected primes and with lexical overlap \citep{jumelet2024,zhou2025icl}, and gradient updates on garden-path sentences improve the fit of RNN surprisal to human adaptation \citep{vanschijndel2018}.
Apart from gradient-based adaptation to garden paths \citep{vanschijndel2018}, these studies use unambiguous primes.

\paragraph{Overridden information in context.}
Information that later context overrides can continue to affect LMs.
Transformer models sometimes refer to discourse entities that a sentence did not introduce \citep{schuster2022}, and models rarely retract their own wrong answers \citep{yang2025retraction} or correct themselves without external feedback \citep{kamoi2024}.
Later tokens retrieve earlier bindings by attending back to the positions where they were encoded \citep{prakash2026}, and activation patching, which replaces internal states with states from a counterfactual input, is the standard tool for locating such information \citep{vig2020causal,meng2022locating,zhang2024patching}.
In human sentence processing, lingering misinterpretations have been attributed to competing representations of the two analyses \citep{slattery2013} and to shallow, good-enough interpretation \citep{ferreira2003}.
Garden paths offer a case of overriding in which the overridden analysis, its strength and the point at which it is overridden are all known.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Materials.}
An item family is one garden-path sentence with its controls.
The discovery set contains 93 NP/Z families, 69 from \citet{amouyal2025} and 24 from the SAP benchmark \citep{huang2024}.
A held-out set of 48 families combines 24 items from \citet{cehakova2025} with 24 from SyntaxGym \citep{gauthier2020}, and was used only after the analyses had been fixed on the discovery set.
A third set contains the 42 optionally transitive items of \citet{christianson2001}, each with a short and a long ambiguous region and a plausible and an implausible object.
Each family provides the primes in Table~\ref{tab:conds}.
\cond{gp} is the garden-path sentence and \cond{cue} its version with a comma after the verb.
\cond{tb} replaces the main-clause subject with a pronoun, so that the noun phrase remains the object.
\cond{nb} and \cond{nc} replace the ambiguous verb with a strictly intransitive one, without and with the comma.

\paragraph{Targets and measures.}
Each prime is followed by a target from every other family that shares no content word with it, which removes lexical repetition and gives each discovery prime 78 to 92 targets.
For a target family with subject $s$, verb $v$ and noun phrase $n$, the production-like measure is
\begin{equation}
L = \log P(\text{\ex{While}}\ s\ v\ n\text{,}) - \log P(\text{\ex{While}}\ s\ v\text{,}\ n),
\end{equation}
where both probabilities are conditioned on the prime and the two strings contain the same words.
$L$ is the log-odds of attaching $n$ as the object of $v$ rather than starting a new clause with it.
The comprehension-like measure $S$ is the surprisal of the first word of the target's main clause after the comma-less fragment \ex{While}~$s$~$v$~$n$, a quantity that contains no punctuation choice.
A high $S$ means that the model expected $n$ to be an object.
For sampled production, the model continues \ex{While}~$s$~$v$, and a rule system over part-of-speech tags codes whether the continuation attaches a noun phrase as an object (Appendix~\ref{app:coding}).

\paragraph{The residue contrast.}
Comparing \cond{gp} with \cond{cue} confounds the abandoned analysis with the comma, and comparing \cond{gp} with \cond{nb} confounds it with the verb.
For any measure $X$ we use
\begin{equation}
A_X = \big(X_{\cond{gp}} - X_{\cond{nb}}\big) - \big(X_{\cond{cue}} - X_{\cond{nc}}\big),
\label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
where $X_c$ is the mean of $X$ over prime families and targets after primes of condition $c$.
Within each bracket the two primes have the same punctuation, and across brackets the same verbs.
$A_X$ is therefore the part of the prime's effect that requires both a missing comma and a verb that can take an object, the condition under which the object analysis is built and then abandoned.
The same contrast with \cond{tb} in place of \cond{gp}, $A_X^{\cond{tb}}$, measures the effect of a noun phrase that really is an object, and we report $r=A_X/A_X^{\cond{tb}}$ as the size of the residue relative to it.

\paragraph{Models and inference.}
We use 24 open-weight causal LMs from the GPT-2, Pythia, Llama, Mistral, Gemma and Qwen families, base and instruction-tuned, between 124M and 70B parameters and including one mixture-of-experts model (Appendix~\ref{app:models}).
22 of them enter every priming experiment, and two more enter the \citet{christianson2001} set.
Scoring uses exact log-probabilities without chat templates, and sampling uses temperature 1 without truncation.
Intervals come from a two-way bootstrap that resamples prime families and target families independently.
For averages across models, each bootstrap draw applies the same resampled families to every model, which keeps the dependence that arises because all models see the same items.
For every confirmatory experiment we fixed predictions and analysis in a dated record before collecting its data, and Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} lists every outcome.

\section{The Abandoned Analysis Persists}
\label{sec:residue}

\paragraph{The residue in production.}
After a garden-path prime, models prefer object attachment in the target more than the controls predict (Figure~\ref{fig:residue}).
On the discovery set $A_L$ is positive in all 22 models and excludes zero in 21, with Pythia-160M as the exception.
Averaged over models it is 0.72 nats, which is 76\% of $A_L^{\cond{tb}}$, the effect of a prime whose noun phrase really is the object.
The residue replicates on the held-out families, where it is positive in all 22 models (20 with intervals above zero) with a median of 0.36 nats, and on the items of \citet{christianson2001}, where it is positive in all 24 models with a median of 1.05 nats.

\paragraph{Punctuation habits.}
A comma-less prime is unusual in written text, and a model could learn from it that this writer omits commas after verbs that can take objects.
Such a habit would raise $L$ without any object analysis.
The \cond{nom} prime keeps the verb and the missing comma of \cond{gp} but replaces the noun phrase with the nominative pronoun \ex{they}, which cannot be an object.
If the residue were a punctuation habit, \cond{gp} and \cond{nom} would prime alike.
Instead, the contrast of Equation~\ref{eq:A} with \cond{nom} and its intransitive counterpart in place of \cond{cue} and \cond{nc} is positive in all 22 models (21 significant, 0.47 nats on average), and across families it grows with the model's expectation of an object after the ambiguous verb in all 22 models.
The case contrast is two thirds of the comma contrast (0.47 against 0.72 nats), so most of the residue requires a noun phrase that could be an object, and the rest follows from the object expected after the verb.

\paragraph{Local string overlap.}
The string \ex{hunted the deer} might prime \ex{washed the baby} by local coherence, as it disrupts human reading even where the grammar excludes an object reading \citep{tabor2004}.
The \cond{pass} prime contains the same string inside a passive clause, where \ex{the deer} cannot be an object.
Its residue has a median of 12\% of the \cond{gp} residue across 22 models, reliably negative in seven, mostly small, models and reliably positive in seven larger ones.
The ratio is higher in the strongest models, 0.45 in Llama-3.1-70B and above 0.5 in the two Qwen2.5-7B models.
In these models a locally computed object reading of the string also leaves a trace.

\paragraph{Sampled production.}
We sampled two continuations of every target fragment after every prime in 12 models, about 57,000 prompts per model.
The residue appears in the proportion of transitive continuations in 11 of 12 models with the pre-specified coding and in all 12 with a refined coding, by 5.0 percentage points on average (Figure~\ref{fig:residue}, right).
Its size across models followed $A_L$ in the eight models available when we specified this prediction (Spearman $\rho=0.81$), and the relation is weaker across all twelve ($\rho=0.48$).
The human raw contrast between garden-path and comma primes was 4.5 points \citep{vangompel2006}, a statistic that cannot be compared directly with LMs (\S\ref{sec:transfer}).

\paragraph{A second garden path.}
In an NP/S garden path (\ex{The student forgot the solution was in the back of the book}) the noun phrase is also first analysed as an object.
We built the same contrast for 64 NP/S families, with \ex{that} as the disambiguating cue and a verb that takes only a clausal complement (\ex{hoped}) as the control, and measured it on the NP/Z targets.
The residue is positive in 19 of 22 models (sign test $p<0.001$) with a pooled value of 0.03 nats.
It is smaller than for NP/Z primes because the prime and target structures differ, and even a real object in an NP/S prime moves the NP/Z targets only by 0.03 to 0.20 nats.

\section{Two Effects with Opposite Signs}
\label{sec:twoforces}


On the comprehension-like measure the residue is small on average (0.05 nats over 22 models) and varies in sign, significantly positive in five models and significantly negative in four.
A negative value means that after a garden-path prime the model expects an object less than the controls predict.
This is what an adaptation toward the resolved structure would produce, and it suggests that a second effect of the prime offsets the residue.

\paragraph{Commitment and error.}
The two accounts of priming tie the effect of a prime to different properties.
Residual activation depends on how strongly the abandoned analysis was built, which we measure as the model's expectation of an object after the ambiguous verb, $\log P(\text{\ex{the}}) - \log P(\text{\ex{,}})$.
Error-driven learning depends on the error at the disambiguation, which we measure as the garden-path strength of the prime, the surprisal of its disambiguating word without the comma minus that with the comma.
The two predictors are only weakly correlated (median $r=0.22$).
In a regression of the family-level residue on both (Figure~\ref{fig:slopes} in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}), object expectation raises the residue on both measures in all 22 models.
Garden-path strength lowers the residue on $S$ in 21 of 22 models, in all 22 when the family's adaptation to the missing comma enters as a covariate (19 significant), whereas on $L$ it raises the residue in most models.
The error effect is specific to garden-path resolutions: on the comma version of the same target, where the disambiguating word is expected, garden-path strength has little effect (median $t=-0.6$, against $-2.8$ on $S$), so the adaptation is not a general loss of confidence after a surprising prime.

\paragraph{Causal tests.}
Garden-path strength varies between families together with other properties.
To manipulate it within a family, we kept each prime up to the noun phrase fixed, which fixes the object analysis, and varied only its continuation, which changes the surprisal at the disambiguation.
More surprising disambiguations reduce the residue on $S$, with $t$ values between $-4.1$ and $-6.4$ in the three models whose between-family analysis had shown an error effect, and near zero in the two models in which it had not.
Surprisal summed over the whole continuation predicts little, so the relevant error is the one at the disambiguation point.
On the items of \citet{christianson2001}, where plausibility changes only the main-clause continuation, the same within-family relation is negative in all ten models that pass the comprehension controls of \S\ref{sec:transfer}.
The residue is tested causally by the patching experiments of \S\ref{sec:mechanism} (see Appendix~\ref{app:e014} for a behavioural manipulation).

\paragraph{Production and comprehension.}
The residue dominates the production-like measure, and the adaptation shows mainly in the comprehension-like one.
We tested this asymmetry prospectively on the items of \citet{christianson2001} with the 16 models available at that time.
The residue on $L$ was positive in all 16, and the error relation was more negative on $S$ than on $L$ in 14.
Human studies examine these two effects in separate paradigms \citep{vangompel2006,fine2013}, whereas in LMs they arise from the same prime.

\paragraph{Other garden paths.}
We tested the two item-level relations on $S$ for two further constructions with targets of the same construction.
For NP/S garden paths neither relation appeared.
For main-verb/reduced-relative garden paths the error relation appeared in six of nine models and the commitment relation in none.
NP/S reanalysis keeps the noun phrase inside the verb's complement, whereas NP/Z and main-verb/reduced-relative reanalysis remove a committed attachment \citep{sturt1996}, which may explain why only the latter produce an adaptation, while the NP/S residue of \S\ref{sec:residue} shows that the abandoned object analysis persists in both.

\section{Where the Residue Lives}
\label{sec:mechanism}

In the garden-path prime, the states of \ex{the deer} are identical to those in the transitive prime \cond{tb}, since the two share every preceding token.
Later tokens can read these states directly, or the main-clause states, which were computed after the disambiguation.
If the residue is read from the noun-phrase states, replacing them with states computed under the subject analysis should remove it, while replacing the main-clause states should not.

\paragraph{Patching design.}
At every layer, the residual-stream states of the noun-phrase tokens in \cond{gp} are replaced with the states of the same tokens in \cond{cue}, where the comma has already made the noun phrase a subject, and the rest of the prime is recomputed.
The target then attends to a mixture of the original and this counterfactual prime: counterfactual keys and values at the noun phrase (NP), at the main clause \ex{ran into the woods.} (MC), or at both, and original ones elsewhere.
The same procedure applied to \cond{nb} with \cond{nc} as donor estimates what the replacement does by importing information about the comma, and Equation~\ref{eq:A} subtracts it.
We report the retained residue $\rho = A_L^{\text{patched}}/A_L$ and the change $\Delta = A_S^{\text{patched}} - A_S$ for nine models between 1.4B and 70B parameters.

\paragraph{The residue is read from the noun phrase.}
Replacing the noun-phrase states leaves 0.01 to 0.71 of the residue (pooled 0.37), and nothing beyond the interval bounds of 0.21 and 0.22 in Qwen2.5-32B and Llama-3.1-70B (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}, left).
Replacing the main-clause states leaves it largely intact (pooled 1.01), and in every model the noun phrase carries more of the residue than the main clause.
With \cond{nb} as donor, where \ex{the deer} follows an intransitive verb without a comma, the retained fraction is still 0.04 to 0.47 in the seven models tested, so the effect is not imported information about the comma.
Replacing the noun-phrase states only in the upper half of the network has no effect in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B, and only in the upper quarter none in Qwen2.5-7B (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}, right).
Later text thus reads the abandoned analysis from the noun phrase's keys and values in the lower and middle layers.

\paragraph{What the noun-phrase states carry.}
Replacing the noun-phrase states with states of the same tokens computed under the object analysis in another context (\ex{It was a quiet morning.} before the prime) leaves 70\% to 111\% of the residue in seven models up to 70B, so the effect of the replacement lies in the analysis it removes.
The states encode that analysis as a role.
A linear probe trained only on noun phrases without a comma, object (\cond{tb}) against subject (\cond{nb}), classifies object and subject noun phrases that follow a comma (\cond{tc}, \cond{cue}) with 96\% to 99\% accuracy in all seven models.
Moving the states along the probe direction toward the subject class removes more of the residue than random directions in three of seven models and less than replacing the whole states in six (Appendix~\ref{app:patching}), so the abandoned analysis is spread over more than one direction of the noun phrase's representation.

\section{Beyond Syntax: Overridden Statements}
\label{sec:facts}

Does content that a context explicitly overrides also keep influencing an LM?
Unlike conflicts between a context and a model's parametric knowledge \citep{longpre2021,xie2024conflict}, both the overridden and the correct value here come from the context.
We built 200 items from ten frames (for example \ex{The meeting is on Thursday. Correction: The meeting is on Monday.}) and measured, after the probe \ex{To confirm, the meeting is on}, how much more likely the overridden value is than the correct one.
A control replaces the overridden statement with the same statement about another entity (\ex{The dinner is on Thursday.}), which matches exposure to the overridden value.
Relative to this control, the overridden value pulls later predictions toward itself by 0.6 to 2.3 nats in eight of nine models up to 70B, in nine or ten of the ten frames each.
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is the exception and moves away from a corrected value.

Attention knockout shows where the answer finds the overridden value.
Blocking the answer's attention to the correcting sentence raises the pull in all nine models, so the knockout works as intended.
In six models, blocking the answer's attention to the overridden statement leaves the pull intact, and blocking the access of all later positions to it removes the pull: the value reaches the answer through the positions after the statement, including the correction itself.
In Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B and Llama-3.1-70B, blocking the answer's own access already halves or removes the pull, so the answer reads the overridden statement directly, as later text reads the abandoned noun phrase in a garden path (Appendix~\ref{app:facts}).
Overridden content thus persists in both settings, and whether it is read from where it was stated or relayed by later positions depends on the model.

\section{Transferring the Human Paradigm}
\label{sec:transfer}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_generation_col}
\caption{Sampled production after garden-path primes. The raw contrast with comma primes used in the human study (orange) is negative in most Llama and Qwen models. Without the completions that are themselves comma-less garden-path sentences it is positive in every model (gray), and so is the controlled residue $A_{gen}$ (blue).}
\label{fig:generation}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The raw statistic reverses.}
The human study compared transitive completions after \cond{gp} and \cond{cue} primes.
In LMs this contrast is reliably positive in only 4 of 12 models and significantly negative in 7 of the 8 Llama and Qwen models, most strongly in Llama-3.1-70B, where garden-path primes lower the proportion of transitive completions by 17.6 points (Figure~\ref{fig:generation}).
After a garden-path prime, up to 29\% of completions are themselves comma-less garden-path sentences, against 0.2\% to 2.4\% after a comma prime.
These completions produce the resolved structure of the prime, a new clause after an object-less verb, in the prime's comma-less style.
They are the adaptation of \S\ref{sec:twoforces} expressed in production, and the raw contrast counts them as evidence against the residue.
Across models, the more such completions a model writes, the more negative its raw contrast ($\rho=-0.82$).
Without them, the raw contrast is positive in all 12 models (median 10 points).
Human participants completed the fragments aloud, without punctuation.
Matching primes for punctuation, as Equation~\ref{eq:A} does, is necessary to transfer the paradigm.

\paragraph{Lingering comprehension is a different measure.}
LMs answer \ex{Did the man hunt the deer?} with yes after the garden-path sentence much more often than after its comma version, and more so when the ambiguous region is longer, as human readers do \citep{christianson2001}.
Across the items of \citet{christianson2001}, however, this lingering is not positively related to the priming residue ($r$ between $-0.30$ and $0.15$ in the ten models that answer the unambiguous control questions correctly), although both measures have split-half reliabilities between 0.77 and 0.98.
Question answering and priming measure different properties of the model, so studies of lingering in LMs \citep{li2024incremental,amouyal2025} and the residue measured here are not interchangeable.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

A garden-path sentence leaves both analyses in an LM's context.
The abandoned one is read from the noun phrase, whose states were computed before the disambiguation, and its effect on later production grows with how strongly it was built.
The resolved one is read from the states after the disambiguation, and its effect grows with the disambiguation error.
Residual activation describes the first effect and error-driven learning the second, with in-context adaptation in the role of learning \citep{zhou2025icl}, and the two representations of the competing-representations view of lingering \citep{slattery2013} occupy different positions of the context.

The patching result was not a foregone conclusion, since later text could have relied on the main-clause states, which are more recent and encode the corrected analysis, and in the 32B and 70B models it reads almost the whole residue from the noun phrase instead.
A disambiguating word or a correcting sentence changes what later positions compute without removing the earlier representation, so appending a correction is a weaker intervention than removing the corrected text, which bears on why models refer to entities a sentence never introduced \citep{schuster2022} and rarely retract wrong answers \citep{yang2025retraction}.
Corrected statements persist in the same way, since eight of nine models still prefer a value after it has been corrected (\S\ref{sec:facts}), so applications that update what a model knows by appending a correction to its context leave the overridden version as an input to later predictions.

For human research, varying only the continuation of a fixed garden-path prefix would separate the two accounts: residual activation predicts no change in later comprehension of garden paths because the abandoned analysis is built identically, whereas error-driven learning predicts more adaptation after more surprising disambiguations, as in LMs.

\section*{Limitations}

We study English NP/Z garden paths in depth and NP/S and main-verb/reduced-relative garden paths in single experiments, so other languages and ambiguity types may behave differently.
Our models are open-weight models up to 70B parameters, because the analyses require exact log-probabilities and access to internal states, and closed models were not tested.
The behavioural manipulation of the abandoned analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:e014}) did not pass its pre-specified check, so the causal evidence for the residue comes from patching and the role-direction intervention, which were run on six to nine of the 24 models.
The contrast of Equation~\ref{eq:A} was defined after inspecting the first two models of the discovery set, and its confirmation rests on held-out items, a prospective test and pre-specified predictions, some of which failed (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}).
The overridden statements are templated, and one pre-specified prediction about their mechanism failed, which led to the different-carrier interpretation of \S\ref{sec:facts}.
The coding of sampled completions is rule-based and was checked against gold sentences and by one annotator on a sample of generations.
We derive a prediction for human readers but do not test it.

This work involves no human participants and no personal data.
All models are public checkpoints used for scoring and sampling on local hardware.
The materials come from published studies and benchmarks and are used for research with citation.
